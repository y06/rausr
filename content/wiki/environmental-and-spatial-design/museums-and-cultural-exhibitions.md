## What it is

Museum and cultural exhibition design turns a collection of objects, research, and voices into an experience a visitor can enter. Graphics contribute orientation, interpretation, atmosphere, and continuity. They should help people understand why an object is here and how it connects to the larger story without competing with the object itself.

Visitors do not all arrive with the same knowledge, time, language, or sensory ability. Some follow the whole route; others see one room or one label. The exhibition should reward both paths. A strong concept creates a recognizable thread while offering more than one way to learn.

## Build the story with the curatorial team

Agree on the central question before choosing a visual style. What should a visitor understand, reconsider, or remember? Map the evidence behind that story: objects, dates, images, testimony, sound, and contested interpretations. Designers, curators, educators, access specialists, conservators, and fabricators need to work together because each choice affects the others.

Write a content hierarchy that separates orientation, section framing, object interpretation, and deeper detail. Decide which information is essential in the gallery and which can live in a handout or digital layer. Avoid asking every label to carry the entire scholarly argument.

## Design for several visitor speeds

### Create a route that can be read

Sketch entrances, sightlines, decision points, rest areas, and likely crowding. At the threshold, a visitor should know what the space is about and where to go next. Section changes should be evident without requiring a dense wall of text. [Wayfinding](/wiki/environmental-and-spatial-design/wayfinding/) becomes part of storytelling when movement through rooms changes the order in which ideas are discovered.

Test the route from standing and seated viewpoints. An object case may hide the next panel; a group can block a narrow label; a projection may wash out information on an adjacent wall. The gallery is a changing environment, not a clean plan view.

### Give information a clear scale

Use a small, consistent hierarchy for titles, introductions, object labels, captions, credits, and optional deeper reading. The viewing distance and lighting determine type size and contrast. A label should be close enough to its object that the relationship is obvious, but far enough from traffic and cases to be comfortably read.

Provide alternatives when important material is visual, auditory, or tactile. The Smithsonian's exhibition guidance explicitly treats access as part of interpretation, including multiple ways to engage with content. Plan this with access specialists and people who use those alternatives rather than adding a last-minute QR code as a substitute.

## Make media earn its place

Audio, film, projections, interactives, and tactile models can reveal things a label cannot. Give each medium a specific purpose. A film might show a process; a model might clarify scale; an audio account might foreground a person's voice. If the same paragraph appears on a wall and a screen, the screen may not add much.

Consider maintenance and failure states. What happens when a screen is off, headphones are missing, or a visitor cannot use a touch interface? Essential interpretation should still be reachable. Check captions, transcripts, control placement, glare, sound spill, and access to seating as part of the design review.

## Prototype in the actual space

Tape the route, place full-size label proofs, and mock up object-to-text relationships before production. Walk it with fresh visitors and ask what they understand without a guide. Observe where they pause, turn back, or miss a connection. A diagram can show a logical sequence; a walk-through shows whether the sequence is experienced.

Coordinate final materials with conservation and facilities teams. Light levels, mounting methods, reflections, temporary fixtures, and cleaning requirements can change the graphic result. Document artwork locations, dimensions, substrate, mounting, lighting assumptions, and replacement files. Make changes traceable when an object or interpretation is updated.

## Practical checks

- Can a visitor understand the exhibition's central idea from the entrance and main section points?
- Are objects, labels, and routes usable from different heights and with varied sensory needs?
- Does each media element add understanding and have an accessible alternative?
- Have full-size graphics been checked in the real lighting and visitor flow?

## Common mistake

Using visual drama to replace interpretation. A spectacular room can attract attention, but visitors need a readable path between the evidence, the story, and their own questions.
