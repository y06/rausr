## What it is

Accessibility is a quality of the complete experience, not a final compliance layer. It depends on design, content, code, device support, and ongoing maintenance. Inclusive design widens the frame by recognising that ability and context vary.

## Core principles

### Perceivable

Information needs alternatives and sufficient distinction. Provide text alternatives for meaningful images, captions for media, adaptable structure, and contrast that remains effective across states.

### Operable

People must be able to complete tasks with different inputs. Keyboard access, visible focus, sufficient target sizes, predictable controls, and alternatives to complex gestures are central concerns.

### Understandable

Use clear language, consistent navigation, explicit labels, helpful errors, and instructions that do not rely only on position, shape, sound, or colour.

### Robust

Semantic structure and correct control behaviour help assistive technologies interpret the interface. Native elements are usually a stronger starting point than custom imitations.

## Practical checks

- Navigate the complete task using only a keyboard.
- Zoom and reflow the interface at narrow widths.
- Check contrast for default, hover, focus, disabled, and error states.
- Test with screen readers and real users, not automated tools alone.
- Respect reduced-motion preferences and avoid unnecessary time limits.

## Common mistake

Treating accessibility as a separate mode for a small audience. Clear hierarchy, captions, forgiving interactions, good contrast, and larger targets help people in bright light, noisy rooms, temporary injury, slow networks, and many everyday situations.
