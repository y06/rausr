## What it is

Art direction for photography defines what an image should communicate and how a set of images should feel together. The art director works with the photographer and production team to make decisions about subjects, location, props, styling, lighting, framing, and final use. The aim is not to control every detail on set; it is to protect the idea while leaving room for photographic discovery.

A single strong photograph is not necessarily a useful visual language. A brand, publication, or campaign often needs images for several channels and stories. Art direction establishes enough consistency that a new image can belong to the same world without repeating the same composition.

## Turn the brief into a visual point of view

Define the audience, message, and role of the photography. Is it evidence of a real process, an emotional portrait, a product explanation, or an atmospheric story? Describe the intended feeling in concrete visual terms. “Warm” might mean low-contrast morning light and close human scale; it should not remain a vague mood-board label.

Collect references to discuss choices, not to copy finished pictures. Separate what you value in each reference—light, colour, gesture, space, texture, or crop. Identify what should be avoided. A clear brief tells the photographer which decisions are essential and where interpretation is welcome.

## Design the image system

### Choose subjects and scenes honestly

Decide what or who needs to appear and why. Consider representation, context, consent, and how people are portrayed. A polished image can still mislead if it suggests a process, place, or product experience that is not real. If the work is conceptual, make its role clear rather than presenting it as documentary evidence.

For product work, plan scale, handling, surface, and relationship to the user. For portraiture, consider the environment and the person's comfort. Styling and props should support the narrative rather than become a catalogue of trends.

### Set visual rules with room to vary

Define preferred light direction, contrast, colour balance, camera distance, perspective, background treatment, and negative space. Plan the crops needed for a hero, a card, a vertical story, and any print use. A composition that fills the whole frame may be beautiful but impossible to place beside text. [Visual identity systems](/wiki/identity-and-branding/visual-identity-systems/) explains how image treatment can become a recognition cue across touchpoints.

Avoid making all images identical. A coherent set can move between wide context, medium interaction, and close detail. The shared point of view should be visible across those shifts.

## Prepare the shoot

Build a shot list around communication needs, not only subject names. For each planned image, record its purpose, orientation, must-have content, likely crop, and approvals. Confirm location, schedule, access, talent, releases, wardrobe, props, and technical requirements with the producer. Allow time for alternate compositions and unexpected useful moments.

On set, compare the live frame with the intended use. Look at background distractions, hands and gestures, edge crops, colour relationships, and whether the main subject still reads at thumbnail size. Check a few images in the actual page or campaign layout before moving on. Small decisions made during capture can save extensive retouching later.

## Select, finish, and hand off

Edit for the story of the whole set. Choose images with distinct roles rather than five similar favourites. Align colour and contrast without erasing the qualities that made the photographs credible. Retouch with restraint and document any changes that could affect an image's truth or product accuracy.

Deliver approved selects, crops, colour-treated masters, export sizes, alt-text guidance, usage rights, and a record of consent or licensing terms where relevant. Name assets so future teams can find the right version. Review the images in context: a photograph that wins on its own may compete with typography or lose its subject beneath a crop.

## Common mistake

Treating the mood board as the finished strategy. References can begin a conversation, but a useful art direction defines why a photographic choice serves the message and how that choice will adapt across a real body of work.
