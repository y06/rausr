## What it is

Editorial illustration is image-making for a piece of published content: a news story, essay, interview, review, column, or feature. It does more than fill a rectangle when photography is unavailable. It can interpret an abstract idea, set a tone, invite curiosity, or help readers see a relationship the writing leaves implicit.

It also carries editorial responsibility. A visual metaphor can make a nuanced argument look absolute, and a convincing scene can be mistaken for a depiction of a real event. The illustration should be appropriate to the article's evidence and point of view, especially when the subject involves identifiable people, health, conflict, or public trust. The image should add a layer of understanding without rewriting the story.

## Read beyond the headline

Read the draft or a reliable synopsis before sketching. Identify the central question, the author's angle, the emotional register, and the facts that must not be distorted. A headline may change late in production; a concept tied only to one clever phrase can become irrelevant. Ask the editor which interpretation the image should support and which tempting interpretations would be misleading.

Collect visual references for the subject, time, place, and cultural context. Be careful with familiar symbols: they may be too generic, culturally narrow, or already loaded with a meaning the story does not intend. Make a small list of visual ideas that have a reason to exist in this particular article, not merely in an article of the same broad topic.

## Find an idea that the text cannot do alone

### Explore metaphor with restraint

Sketch several routes: a direct scene, a conceptual metaphor, a diagrammatic relationship, or a character-led moment. Ask what each adds. A metaphor should clarify a tension or reveal a fresh connection, not turn a complex issue into a misleading visual shortcut. If a subject is sensitive, discuss the interpretation with the editor before the image becomes polished.

Test each rough at the size and crop in which readers will first see it. A subtle visual idea may work across a printed spread but vanish as a small homepage thumbnail. A literal scene may be immediately readable but add nothing to the story. Choose the route that best balances meaning, legibility, and editorial tone.

### Design with the article, not beside it

Know the dimensions, orientation, crop rules, colour treatment, and space needed for headlines, captions, or overlays. A strong illustration can fail when the page design cuts off the visual hinge of the idea. Work with the art director or designer on composition while there is still room to change it. [Magazines and newspapers](/wiki/editorial-and-publication/magazines-and-newspapers/) and [long-form typography](/wiki/editorial-and-publication/long-form-typography/) provide the surrounding editorial system.

Decide whether the work needs one hero illustration, a related series, or a flexible set of crops. Keep the visual language coherent, but let each image answer its own passage. For digital publication, check thumbnail and social-preview crops as well as the full article placement.

## Brief and commission clearly

A good brief names the story, audience, intended use, dimensions, deadline, approval stages, and any factual or visual restrictions. It also leaves room for the illustrator to solve the problem. Specify the number of roughs and revision rounds, who gives consolidated feedback, and what files are needed at delivery. The Association of Illustrators' commissioning guidance offers a useful checklist.

Agree usage terms before work begins. Editorial use in one edition is different from indefinite use across advertising, merchandise, and new territories. The scope of any licence should reflect the actual commission, and questions about ownership or permissions should be resolved in the agreement rather than assumed from the delivered file. This is a production conversation, not a decorative footnote.

## Review the published image

Check the illustration in the final page or screen, not only as an isolated artwork. Does it still support the article after headline and copy edits? Is the caption or credit correct? Is the image described appropriately for people who cannot see it, without simply repeating the headline? For a conceptual image, useful alternative text can name the visible metaphor and its relationship to the story.

Keep approved artwork, usage terms, source files, and final exports organised. If the article is later republished or promoted in a new context, the team should know what was approved and whether additional use needs agreement.

## Practical checks

- Does the image offer an interpretation specific to this story?
- Could the visual be mistaken for documentary evidence or an unsupported claim?
- Has the illustration been tested with its headline, crop, and page design?
- Are feedback, deadline, deliverables, and usage terms agreed in advance?
- Do credits and alternative text remain accurate in every published placement?

## Common mistake

Commissioning “something creative” without explaining the editorial problem, then rejecting sketches for not matching an unspoken expectation. A precise brief can protect both the story and the illustrator's freedom to find an unexpected answer.
