## What it is

An icon compresses a concept into a small visual form. A system becomes coherent when icons share decisions about perspective, stroke, fill, corner treatment, proportion, detail, and visual weight.

## Meaning before style

Start by defining the concept and context. Some metaphors are widely learned; others are local, technical, or obsolete. Similar actions need clearly different silhouettes, while related objects should share a visible family resemblance.

## Build the visual grammar

A grid provides coordination but should not replace optical correction. Circles, diagonals, and dense shapes often need slight adjustments to appear equal in size and weight. Define how strokes end, how corners turn, when shapes overlap, and how much detail survives at the smallest target size.

## Test the set

Icons should be reviewed together, in the real interface or environment, at final size. Test unfamiliar symbols without labels to learn what people infer; then add text where recognition or safety matters. Labels are especially important for uncommon actions and abstract states.

## Practical checks

- Compare visual weight rather than only geometric dimensions.
- Test filled, outlined, active, disabled, and reversed states where required.
- Keep touch targets larger than the visible glyph in interactive contexts.
- Supply meaningful accessible names for interactive icons.
- Avoid using tiny detail as the only distinction between two actions.

## Common mistake

Assuming that a clean drawing is automatically universal. Icons are learned signs. Even familiar symbols can change meaning across cultures, industries, generations, and products, so context and testing remain essential.
