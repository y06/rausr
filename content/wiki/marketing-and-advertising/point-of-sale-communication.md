## What it is

Point-of-sale communication is the layer of messages a shopper encounters near a product or transaction: shelf cards, display headers, counter units, window prompts, digital screens, and temporary offers. It is distinct from a store's permanent wayfinding and from packaging, although all three must work together. Its job is to clarify a decision at a particular location and moment.

In-store attention is fragmented. People are comparing products, moving around others, talking to staff, or following a shopping list. A message that takes thirty seconds to decode in a design review may never be read in the aisle.

## Begin with the choice being made

Define the behaviour the communication should support: find a new product, understand a difference, notice a valid offer, or complete an action. Identify the evidence that makes the claim credible. A discount headline without clear terms can create mistrust; a beautiful display that obscures the product can work against the sale.

Observe the actual store or a representative mock-up. Record sightlines, shelving height, competing promotions, product facings, lighting, replenishment, and how long the piece will be installed. The best placement may be a small shelf-level cue rather than a large freestanding structure.

## Make the message quick to use

### Build a three-level hierarchy

Lead with the relevant product or benefit, then the reason to believe it, then a clear action or essential condition. Use plain, specific language. Test the display from several metres, at shelf distance, and close enough to read details. Text size and contrast should fit those viewing conditions rather than a screen mock-up.

Let packaging remain identifiable. If the POS system changes the brand colour or hides the variant labels, shoppers have to work harder to connect the message to the product. Use a consistent graphic cue across related displays while adapting to the shape and density of each store location.

### Design for the operating store

Choose materials, attachments, and replacement methods with store staff. A display must not block access, create a trip hazard, conceal mandatory information, or prevent replenishment. Temporary campaigns need an installation and removal plan. Digital displays need a readable static fallback if the screen is off or the content fails.

Check claims, dates, prices, and promotional conditions with the people responsible for them. These rules vary by market and category; a design guide cannot replace legal review. Keep a clear source of truth for copy so that reprints and regional versions do not quietly diverge.

## Prototype and learn

Build a full-size sample. Put it beside real products and other store graphics. Ask people what they noticed first, which product the message belongs to, and what they think the offer means. Watch whether they can reach the product and whether staff can refill the display. Revisit after installation to see what actually survives the first week.

At handoff, provide dimensioned artwork, materials, fixing instructions, approved copy, version dates, replacement parts, and an owner for removal. A successful POS system should be easy to install correctly and hard to leave up after it becomes inaccurate.

## Common mistake

Designing for maximum visual noise. More colour, more claims, and larger units do not necessarily make a choice easier. The strongest point-of-sale message is often the one that removes a specific uncertainty at exactly the right moment.
