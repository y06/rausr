## What it is

Posters and out-of-home formats place a public message in a physical environment. They range from cultural posters viewed at walking speed to large roadside boards seen for only a few seconds. The design has to compete with movement, weather, architecture, other messages, and the viewer's reason for being there.

A poster can reward a second look. A roadside execution often cannot. The same campaign idea may need different amounts of detail for those two conditions while retaining a recognisable visual code.

## Design for the viewing condition

The format alone does not define the problem. Distance, speed, lighting, placement, dwell time, and visual noise determine how much information can be understood. Begin with a placement inventory: dimensions, orientation, sightline, likely viewing distance, restrictions, and whether the viewer is walking, waiting, or passing in a vehicle.

Decide what a person should take away from one encounter. That may be a name, an event date, a distinctive product, or a simple call to action. Supporting detail should be available where people have time to act on it, such as a nearby sign, landing page, or programme.

### Hierarchy

A strong outdoor piece usually has one dominant entry point, one supporting idea, and a clear source or action. The relationship between image and words should be immediate: they can reinforce each other or create a useful surprise, but the viewer should not need a paragraph of explanation to resolve it.

Put the most important message at the point of strongest visibility. Give dates, locations, URLs, and legal copy the space their role requires. If every element asks for equal attention, none receives it.

### Scale and contrast

Artwork should be evaluated at an approximation of the intended viewing distance. Small desktop previews encourage detail that disappears in context. Strong figure–ground separation and generous spacing usually outperform delicate distinctions. Check the design in daylight, low light, and alongside the colours of its surroundings.

Size is relative to the viewing situation. A headline that fills a laptop screen can still be tiny on a street poster. Print a reduced mock-up, step back, and ask what remains visible after a quick glance.

### Campaign consistency

A family of placements needs a repeated code: composition, colour, type, image treatment, character, or verbal device. The code creates recognition while individual executions keep the campaign fresh. Define which elements are fixed, which may change, and how the layout responds to very narrow or very wide formats.

Motion or digital screens add time as another constraint. The key message must remain understandable without waiting through a complete sequence, and critical information should not depend on a transition that someone may miss.

## Production and placement

Work from the supplier's actual specification, including bleed, safe areas, colour profile, resolution, and finishing requirements. Panel joints, frames, crops, and obstructions can cut through important content. A street photograph or scaled placement mock-up is more informative than a clean artboard alone.

Check who controls updates and removal. A beautiful event poster with an outdated date damages clarity quickly; a campaign system should make replacement and adaptation straightforward.

## Practical checks

- Can the main message be identified after a brief glance?
- Is the advertiser or organiser unmistakable?
- Does the design work in the worst likely light and location?
- Are critical details accurate, legible, and placed inside safe production areas?
- Can the idea adapt to multiple aspect ratios without losing its hierarchy?

## Common mistake

Treating a billboard as an enlarged leaflet. Increasing the dimensions does not increase attention time. Outdoor design usually becomes stronger as the number of simultaneous messages decreases and the relationship between them becomes clearer.
