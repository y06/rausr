## What it is

An explainer animation helps someone understand an unfamiliar idea, product, process, or decision. It can show states changing, parts connecting, causes producing effects, or a route through a service. The movement is useful only when it helps the viewer build a mental model. An animated sequence of decorative icons may look lively while explaining very little.

The audience may encounter an explainer on a landing page, in a presentation, on social media, or inside a product. Each context changes how much attention, sound, and control are available. Define the one useful understanding the viewer should leave with before deciding how long the animation should be.

## Write for the viewer's question

Begin with the question the audience actually has. What are they confused about? What action or decision follows understanding? Gather the facts from subject experts and distinguish essential information from supporting detail. A short film cannot carry every feature, qualification, and exception; it needs a focused through-line and a clear route to deeper information.

Draft a simple outline: situation, change or mechanism, consequence, and next step. Then read the script aloud at a natural pace. Spoken language usually needs shorter sentences and clearer transitions than a written article. If a concept requires several new terms, introduce them one at a time and show what each term refers to before moving on.

## Design the visual explanation

### Show relationships, not just objects

Translate the script into visible changes. A diagram can reveal where information moves; a transformation can show before and after; a repeated shape can help viewers track one actor through a process. Use labels when they reduce ambiguity. Avoid cutting away from an important relationship at the very moment it becomes understandable.

Choose a visual grammar for colour, shape, perspective, type, and motion. It should connect to the organisation's [motion identity](/wiki/motion-and-time-based-design/motion-identity/) without making brand effects more important than the lesson. Use consistent cues for the same entity or state, and reserve strong motion for moments that carry meaning.

### Storyboard before polishing

Build rough frames with the script beside them. Mark what the viewer needs to notice, what changes, and how one frame leads to the next. An animatic with simple timing and temporary audio is often more revealing than a polished set of stills: it shows whether the explanation is too fast, whether a transition hides a logical step, and whether text stays on screen long enough to read.

Test the animatic with someone unfamiliar with the subject. Ask them to explain the process in their own words. If they can remember the visual style but not the mechanism, simplify or reorder the story.

## Treat sound and silence as two real contexts

Narration, music, and effects can support pace and emphasis, but the core meaning should not disappear when sound is unavailable. Add accurate captions for meaningful speech and sound. Where important information exists only in the visuals, plan how it will be conveyed through narration, audio description, or another accessible text route. These choices are easier to make while scripting than after export; the W3C media guidance in the sources below is a practical planning reference.

Check contrast and legibility at the smallest intended display size. Keep on-screen copy brief enough to read at the chosen pace. Avoid rapid flashes and unnecessary camera movement. For web contexts with interactive or looping motion, respect reduced-motion preferences where applicable and give viewers a way to pause or stop non-essential animation.

## Produce for reuse and revision

Separate narration, music, captions, text, and visual layers so updates do not require rebuilding the entire piece. Keep editable vector and animation sources organised. Confirm licences for voice, sound, type, imagery, and any commissioned illustration, including the intended distribution channels.

Export for the real placements rather than assuming one master crop fits every aspect ratio. Review a phone-sized version, a silent version, and the version embedded in its final page. Check whether the first frame sets context and whether the final frame gives a useful next step without rushing the viewer.

## Practical checks

- Can a viewer explain the main mechanism after one viewing?
- Does each motion cue clarify a change, relationship, or transition?
- Are captions, visual alternatives, and readable text planned from the start?
- Does the story still work when sound is off or the frame is small?
- Can the team update facts, language, and formats from organised source files?

## Common mistake

Using movement to disguise an unclear script. If the underlying explanation is not sound, more transitions will only make the confusion harder to pause and inspect. Fix the sequence of ideas first, then animate the parts that benefit from time.
