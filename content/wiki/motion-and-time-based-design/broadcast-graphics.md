## What it is

Broadcast graphics are the visual information layers that accompany video: opening sequences, lower thirds, captions, score or data panels, transitions, stings, bugs, and end cards. They can establish a programme's identity, but their first duty is to make changing information intelligible without taking attention away from the footage.

Unlike a one-off film title, a broadcast package often has to be reused by editors or a live production team. Names change, stories run long, feeds arrive late, and the same programme may appear on a television, phone, and social clip. A good system anticipates those conditions.

## Define the editorial roles

Inventory every graphic that production actually needs. Separate identity moments from information moments. An opener can afford atmosphere; a speaker's name needs immediate recognition; an urgent correction needs clarity and accuracy. List content sources, approval owners, required updates, and the expected on-screen duration of each element.

Work with editors and producers on the visual hierarchy. What must viewers notice first when a lower third appears over a busy scene? How does a graphic enter and leave without obscuring an important action or subtitle? Test with representative footage, not a neutral placeholder.

## Build a system for real content

### Typography and safe framing

Use type that remains legible at the smallest likely viewing size and over varied imagery. Check long names, roles, multiple lines, and different scripts before approving a template. Provide contrast strategies for light and dark footage. Keep essential information within the safe areas required by the production and distribution specifications; these vary, so obtain the actual delivery requirements.

Avoid putting too much into one lower third. If a role and name cannot be read in the available time, simplify the content or give it another presentation. Use animation to establish where information comes from and where it goes, not to make routine facts theatrical.

### Template the right controls

Expose the fields operators need—name, role, colour variant, timing, or panel count—while locking fragile construction details. Test the longest plausible data and empty fields. A motion template that breaks when an editor changes the text is not a production-ready component.

Define a naming convention and versioning process so that teams can tell which asset is current. Provide rendered fallback elements for systems or venues that cannot run the editable template. Check font licensing and whether the destination workstation has the required assets.

## Check time, format, and accuracy

Review graphics in motion at normal playback speed, with sound and captions present. Check the first and last frame, not only a still from the middle. Test 16:9 and intended crops separately; a vertical social cut may need a recomposed information layer rather than a centre crop.

For live information, establish a verification path and a correction procedure. A beautiful statistic or nameplate becomes harmful when it is wrong. Specify the data source, who enters it, who approves it, and how a correction can be made quickly on air or in a later edit.

## Handoff and common mistake

Deliver templates, usage rules, timing examples, safe-area guides, font and asset notes, test content, and a contact for updates. Let an editor outside the design team make a real lower third before sign-off.

The common mistake is presenting a polished opening animation as the whole package. The daily lower thirds, corrections, and format variants reveal whether the identity can actually live in production.
