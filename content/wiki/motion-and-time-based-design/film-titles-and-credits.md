## What it is

Film titles are part of the viewing experience, not decoration placed before the film. They can establish atmosphere, introduce a world, or quietly give the story room to begin. Credits identify contributors and production entities. Their order and wording are often controlled by production agreements, so visual design must work with an authoritative approved list rather than treating names as placeholder copy.

Opening titles and end credits have different rhythms. An opening may carry narrative tension; a closing sequence may provide space for acknowledgement after the final scene. Both need to remain readable on the screens and versions where the work will actually appear.

## Find the title's role in the story

Watch the relevant footage and discuss the director's intent. What should the audience know before the first scene? What should remain unresolved? Explore routes through type, imagery, editing, and sound rather than beginning with a fashionable effect. A title can be a single confident card; complexity is useful only when it sharpens the film's voice.

Storyboard the sequence with its approximate duration and transition into or out of the footage. Test titles over representative shots, including bright, dark, busy, and moving backgrounds. If the title treatment depends on footage that may change, agree on a replacement strategy early.

## Design for time and legibility

### Typography in motion

Choose type for character and performance. Thin strokes, small sizes, extreme letter spacing, and low contrast can fail in motion even when they look elegant in a still. Read each card at playback speed and from a normal viewing distance. Motion should guide attention, not force viewers to chase information across the frame.

Allow time to read names and roles. Test long names, multiple languages, and the most crowded credit card before locking the style. Preserve visual hierarchy between the film title, principal credits, and the larger end-credit list. Do not assume a single font size solves every viewing environment.

### Sound, versions, and accessibility

Coordinate cues with the edit and sound design. An animation that lands perfectly in silence may feel wrong after the music changes. Keep a version history tied to the approved picture and credit list. Check requirements for theatrical, streaming, social, and international deliveries with the producer or distributor; crop and safe-area conventions may differ.

Titles containing meaningful information may need corresponding accessible text or description in the delivery workflow. Coordinate with captioning and accessibility teams rather than expecting the moving image alone to carry every name or narrative clue. Provide reduced-flash or other alternatives where the project calls for them.

## Manage credit accuracy as design data

Use a single approved source for names, roles, spelling, order, and required marks. Assign a production owner for approvals. When a revision arrives, compare it against the prior list and regenerate or reproof every affected card. A misspelled credit is not a small typographic imperfection; it is an inaccurate acknowledgement.

Deliver editable project files, fonts or licensing notes, renders in required formats, cue sheet, colour and frame specifications, and a final approved credit list. Check a full-speed playback and a frame-by-frame technical pass. Make sure the sequence enters and exits the surrounding footage cleanly.

## Common mistake

Polishing a beautiful title animation before the edit, credit data, and delivery formats are stable. Begin with story role and production constraints; then make the motion feel inevitable rather than bolted on.
