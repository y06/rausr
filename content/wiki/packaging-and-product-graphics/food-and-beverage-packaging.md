## What it is

Food and beverage packaging is both a communication surface and a physical product interface. It carries identity, appetite appeal, product description, quantity, ingredients, instructions, regulatory information, and production marks within a constrained structure.

## Build the front-of-pack hierarchy

The shopper should be able to answer a sequence of questions quickly: who made it, what is it, which variant is it, and why might it be relevant? The order changes by category. A familiar brand may lead with its name; a new product may need to lead with the product type or benefit.

## Design the range, not only one pack

A single variant can hide system problems. Test the full expected range, including the longest names, closest flavours, promotional flashes, and future extensions. Stable brand elements create recognition; controlled variable elements make selection easier.

## Connect graphics and structure

Dielines, folds, seams, closures, curvature, transparency, and print processes affect the composition. Critical information should not cross unreliable areas. Mock-ups help reveal what is visible when the pack is held, stacked, chilled, or placed on a shelf.

## Practical checks

- Review the design at actual size and at realistic shelf distance.
- Confirm mandatory information with the responsible regulatory and production teams.
- Test colour and contrast on the intended substrate and print process.
- Check barcode quiet zones, batch areas, recycling marks, and variable-data needs.
- Ensure variant coding remains understandable for people with colour-vision differences.

## Common mistake

Designing the front panel as a standalone poster. Packaging is viewed from several angles, touched, opened, stored, and compared with adjacent products. The complete object is the interface.
