## What it is

A label is a printed surface applied to a product or container; a sleeve may wrap around, slide over, or shrink to fit it. Both turn a three-dimensional object into a communication surface. Their design has to work when the pack is picked up, rotated, chilled, handled, scanned, and placed among competitors—not only when a flat artwork file is viewed at 100%.

The label's job changes by context. It may need to make a brand recognisable, distinguish a variant, show how to use the product, and carry information required by its category and market. This guide focuses on graphic and production decisions, not on giving a universal list of legal label requirements. Confirm those requirements with the product owner and appropriate specialists before artwork approval.

## Begin with the actual container

Measure the object and obtain an approved dieline or application specification from the printer or converter. Note tapers, shoulders, seams, caps, grip areas, curved surfaces, and any region that will distort during application. Photograph the product from the angles a customer will see: on a shelf, in a hand, in an e-commerce thumbnail, and after opening.

Map the information that must be present. Separate product identity and selection cues from instructions, claims, quantity, ingredients or materials, traceability, and codes. The amount and legal status of this information depend on the product; a fictional placeholder should never be treated as production copy. Agree who owns each field and how late changes will be approved.

## Design the viewing sequence

### Make the first glance useful

Decide what a person should recognise first, second, and after turning the pack. A front-facing panel may be only a portion of the total wrap. Put the product name and important variant distinction where they remain visible in normal display. Use colour, type, image, and shape as a coordinated hierarchy rather than relying on a single coloured stripe.

Related products need a shared logic. Stable brand cues and changing variant cues should coexist; [food and beverage packaging](/wiki/packaging-and-product-graphics/food-and-beverage-packaging/) shows one context where that balance matters. When a range grows, test adjacent variants together; similarity that looks elegant in a presentation can become a picking error on a busy shelf.

### Give detailed information room to breathe

Small type on a curved or textured surface is harder to read than the same type on a flat page. Test at real size, in the intended light, and on the actual material. Keep important information away from overlaps, folds, seams, or areas distorted by shrink. Avoid splitting a sentence across a visible join. If the container gives too little space, revisit the structure or information plan rather than shrinking everything to fit.

Design the back and side panels as carefully as the front. Group related details and create a predictable reading order. Use symbols only when their meaning is established for the product and market. A QR code can extend information, but it should not be used as an excuse to hide information that must remain on the physical pack.

## Prototype materials and print

Choose the label stock, adhesive, finish, and print method with the supplier. Transparency, metallic ink, textured paper, glossy films, and shrink materials can all change contrast and colour. A white layer may be needed behind print on a clear substrate; a sleeve may require distortion compensation. These are production decisions to test, not effects to simulate only in a mock-up.

Reserve space for barcodes and human-readable numbers early. GS1 guidance covers placement and scanning considerations for different package forms. Curvature, folds, glare, substrate transparency, and quiet zones can affect the result. Verify the final printed code on an applied sample with the supplier or scanning team rather than assuming a successful on-screen preview proves it works.

Use accurate proofs: a flat print proof, an applied prototype, and a photograph at shelf distance. Check alignment around the wrap, colour consistency, adhesive edges, scuffing, and what happens after refrigeration or handling if those conditions matter. Review the final artwork against the approved copy and the latest dieline.

## Prepare for updates and variants

Build a master file with named layers and controlled text fields. Keep the product name, variant, quantity, code, and mandatory information distinct so that a new SKU can be made without copying errors. Record substrate, profile, supplier, dieline version, and proof approvals. A well-organised handoff is part of the design system.

When the pack changes shape or size, do not simply scale the artwork. Re-evaluate hierarchy, barcode position, type size, and the first-glance view. The label is successful only when it works on the new object.

## Practical checks

- Is the approved dieline based on the real container and application method?
- Can the product and variant be identified from the normal display angle?
- Is detailed copy readable on the printed, applied material?
- Have seams, curvature, shrink, transparency, and glare been tested?
- Are code placement, print quality, and final copy approved by the right owners?

## Common mistake

Approving a beautiful flat rectangle and discovering too late that the container bends, hides, or distorts the important part. Prototype on the actual object before treating the artwork as finished.
