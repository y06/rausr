## What it is

Structural packaging graphics are visual decisions made in response to a package's physical form. A flat dieline becomes a carton, sleeve, pouch, tube, or label wrapped around an object. Folds alter alignment; curves change apparent type size; seals and closures hide parts of the design. Graphics have to cooperate with engineering, materials, filling, transport, and opening.

This guide focuses on that physical interface. The wider questions of product positioning and range architecture belong to consumer packaging and packaging-family design; here the critical question is whether the artwork works *after the structure becomes real*.

## Read the structure before designing

Obtain the current approved dieline from the structural or print partner. Mark front, back, top, bottom, glue area, seams, folds, cut-outs, windows, and any panel that may be covered in store. Confirm what is measured from an inside or outside face. An old dieline with the same nominal size is not a safe substitute for the production file.

Make a plain paper prototype and handle it. Which panel faces forward on a shelf? Where does a thumb land when the pack is opened? What rotates out of view on a curved container? Which surfaces must carry product information even if a sticker is added later? Answer these questions before spending time on fine typography.

## Compose across three dimensions

### Establish the visible hierarchy

Place identity, product type, variant, and essential information on the faces people are most likely to encounter. Use wraparound elements deliberately: a continuous pattern can unify the object, but a critical word should not break unpredictably at a fold. Keep fine details away from glue flaps, heat seals, and areas likely to distort.

Artwork should account for physical tolerances. Ask the printer for bleed, registration, trapping, minimum stroke, and colour guidance appropriate to the process. Metallic or transparent substrates can change contrast. A screen rendering may communicate intent, but only a sample on the intended material can confirm readability and finish.

### Protect operational information

Reserve space for codes, batch or date marks, required copy, and later labels. Check these placements with the relevant specialists. A barcode over a fold, corner, or damaged area may not scan reliably; GS1 guidance should be consulted for the package type and market. Do not relocate compliance information merely to save the visual composition.

Prototype the longest ingredient list, most crowded translation, and smallest package size. A system that works only on the flagship pack is not ready for production.

## Proof the object, not just the PDF

Review artwork at 100% and on a folded mock-up. Check orientation, fold direction, seam joins, type near edges, window alignment, and whether content appears upside down after assembly. Then review a printer or converter proof, followed by a production sample when the run justifies it. Photograph the sample in shelf and in-hand conditions.

Maintain a controlled source of truth for dieline revision, copy, artwork version, and material specification. Changes to structure can invalidate graphic decisions; changes to copy can break a previously safe panel. Set a sign-off sequence with structural, legal, and print partners rather than assuming one visual approval covers every risk.

## Common mistake

Treating the dieline as a canvas and the 3D mock-up as proof. The real package has tolerances, material behaviour, and viewing angles. Design and test those together, then let the flat file document the approved object.
