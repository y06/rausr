## What it is

A name is a compact verbal identifier. Verbal identity is the wider set of choices that makes a brand recognisable in language: vocabulary, sentence rhythm, point of view, message hierarchy, and the way it addresses people. It appears in navigation labels and error messages as much as in campaigns. Neither a clever name nor a witty headline can compensate for an unclear offer.

The best system gives writers room to respond naturally while keeping the same underlying character. It is not a collection of catchphrases to paste everywhere. A customer support reply, product explanation, and recruitment page have different jobs; they should still feel as though they come from the same organisation.

## Start with the naming problem

Define what the name must identify: a company, product, service, place, or programme. List where it will appear and who must understand, remember, search for, or pronounce it. Record existing equity before proposing a replacement. A familiar but imperfect name may be more valuable than a fresh option that nobody recognises.

Build a brief with constraints that can actually rule ideas in or out: languages, likely future products, possible abbreviations, partner names, URL and social use, and the competitive vocabulary in the category. Separate requirements from preferences. “Must work when spoken over the phone” is testable; “should feel premium” needs examples of what that means.

## Develop and test territories

### Generate distinct routes

Explore different naming territories rather than dozens of slight variations on one word. A descriptive route explains the offer; an evocative one suggests a feeling or idea; an invented name may give more room to establish meaning. None is inherently better. Judge how each supports the positioning and how much explanation it needs at launch.

Shortlist names in context: a search result, an app listing, a receipt, a sign, and a sentence spoken by another person. Look for unintended readings, difficult spelling, uncomfortable sound combinations, and names that collapse into generic category language. Test with people who were not in the naming workshop, including speakers of relevant languages.

### Treat clearance as a separate gate

Initial web and registry searches can expose obvious conflicts, but they are not legal clearance. Names are assessed in relation to goods, services, and jurisdictions. Ask qualified trademark advisers to review a serious shortlist before final approval; check domains and handles as operational questions too. Do not build an identity around one favourite name and hope conflicts disappear.

## Build the voice around real tasks

Write a few voice principles in observable terms. “Clear before clever” can mean leading with the action, avoiding invented jargon, and explaining the benefit before a metaphor. Pair each principle with a useful *do* and *avoid* example. Then test it on three unlike pieces of writing: a homepage introduction, a transactional message, and a response to a problem.

Tone can vary with context without changing the voice. A delivery error should be more direct than a celebratory campaign. Define a small message hierarchy—what we do, why it matters, evidence, and action—so copywriters know what to prioritise when space is tight. Create terminology rules for product names, capitalisation, inclusive language, and words that could mislead.

## Handoff and quality checks

Deliver the approved name with pronunciation, spelling, permitted short forms, and examples of how it works beside product or service names. Provide voice principles, before-and-after copy examples, reusable message patterns, and an owner who can resolve new cases. Review real pages after launch: if every team invents a different tone, the guide may be too abstract.

- Can a new writer produce a helpful error message without copying a slogan?
- Does the name still work when spoken, searched, translated, and reduced to a small interface label?
- Are legal and operational checks complete before a public reveal?

## Common mistake

Choosing a name only because it sounds exciting in a presentation. The name has to withstand repetition, search, conversation, and expansion. Meaning is built through consistent experience; verbal identity gives that experience a language people can recognise.
