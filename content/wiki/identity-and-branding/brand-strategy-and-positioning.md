## What it is

Brand strategy is a set of choices about whom an organisation serves, what it offers, what it stands for, and how it should behave. Positioning is the focused place the brand wants to occupy relative to the alternatives people consider. Neither is a colour palette or a slogan. They are the decisions that make later design choices easier to explain and harder to reduce to personal taste.

Strategy is useful when it changes action. A statement such as “innovative and customer-focused” rarely helps a designer choose between two layouts or a team decide which product message to lead with. A stronger strategy names a particular audience problem, a credible difference, and the trade-offs the organisation is willing to make.

## Investigate the real context

Begin with evidence from customers, staff, products, services, competitors, and culture. Listen to the words people use when they describe the category and what they expect from it. Examine where the organisation already behaves consistently and where it makes contradictory promises. Look beyond direct competitors: the audience may compare a product with a different way of solving the same job.

Separate observations from assumptions. A founder's ambition, a sales team's repeated objection, and a customer interview are different kinds of evidence. Record the source of each insight and the questions still unanswered. Research need not become a giant report; it should make the design problem more precise. The Design Council's discover–define–develop–deliver framework is a useful reminder to understand the challenge before converging on a solution.

## Make the positioning specific

Describe the audience in terms of need and situation, not only age or profession. What are they trying to achieve? What makes the current options frustrating? What would give them reason to believe this organisation? A good positioning statement can be written in plain language: for whom, in which context, the brand offers what value, and on what credible basis.

### Find the difference that matters

Difference is not automatically a virtue. An unusual visual style may be memorable but irrelevant to the audience's choice. Ask whether the proposed distinction is useful, believable, sustainable, and visible in the actual experience. If it depends on a claim the organisation cannot consistently deliver, the design will be asked to cover an operational gap.

Map competing promises and conventions without assuming that every convention should be broken. Familiar category cues can help recognition; a deliberate departure can signal a meaningful difference. Decide which cues to keep, which to change, and why. This helps the later [visual identity system](/wiki/identity-and-branding/visual-identity-systems/) stand out for a reason.

### State the trade-offs

Strategy becomes sharper when it says what the brand is not trying to be. A premium specialist cannot always communicate like a mass-market bargain. A calm public service may not need a constantly playful tone. Name the choices that would dilute the positioning, and test them against genuine business constraints rather than treating a workshop phrase as permanent truth.

## Translate strategy into a design brief

Give designers a small set of usable principles: desired impression, key audience tasks, proof points, tone, non-negotiable constraints, and examples of situations the identity must handle. Show what the strategy means for messaging hierarchy, imagery, typography, motion, and service behaviour. Principles should guide judgment, not prescribe every asset before exploration begins.

Prototype more than one route. A concept may express the positioning through clarity, warmth, technical confidence, or another relevant quality. Test routes in realistic applications—small screens, long product names, confusing moments, low-cost production—not only in a hero presentation. Later [brand guidelines](/wiki/identity-and-branding/brand-guidelines/) can document what survives these tests.

## Validate and maintain the position

Ask people unfamiliar with the project what they infer from the proposed language and design. Compare their answers with the intended position, but do not expect a single image to communicate the entire strategy. Look for repeated patterns across touchpoints and interactions. The visual system should support the experience people actually receive.

Revisit positioning when the audience, offer, or competitive context changes. Preserve recognition where it still helps; change what no longer reflects the organisation. A strategy is a working hypothesis tested by behaviour, not a ceremonial deck filed away after launch.

## Practical checks

- Can the audience and their problem be described without generic demographic shorthand?
- Is the claimed difference relevant and supported by real evidence?
- Does the strategy identify useful trade-offs and decision rules?
- Can a designer apply it to both a hero moment and an awkward everyday format?
- Do people experience the promise in the product or service, not only in the advertising?

## Common mistake

Treating a positioning sentence as the finished strategy. The real test is whether it helps teams make consistent choices and whether the organisation can deliver the promise those choices communicate.
