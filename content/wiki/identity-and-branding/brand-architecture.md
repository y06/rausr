## What it is

Brand architecture is the logic connecting an organisation to its products, services, divisions, and acquired names. It answers an audience question: “What is this, and how does it relate to the thing I already know?” The answer influences naming, endorsement, navigation, visual identity, packaging, sales materials, and support.

A branded house puts a prominent parent identity across many offers. A house of brands gives individual names more independence. Between them are endorsed and hybrid systems. These are not style presets to choose from a diagram; they are relationships that must be legible in real encounters.

## Map what exists before drawing a model

Inventory every live name, logo, product tier, programme, and partnership. Record who owns it, who uses it, the audience it serves, and where it appears. Mark names with strong recognition and those that survive only because no one has retired them. A spreadsheet can expose overlaps that a polished brand-family slide hides.

Ask how customers describe the portfolio without seeing the organisation chart. Do they know whether two products work together, whether a service comes from the parent company, or whether a renamed product is the same one they used before? Internal reporting lines rarely form a good public-facing architecture by themselves.

## Choose the relationship, then express it

### Make the strategic decision explicit

For each offer, decide whether the parent should lead, endorse, sit quietly behind it, or remain absent. The decision should reflect audience familiarity, difference in positioning, risk, distribution, and the cost of managing another distinct identity. A separate logo is not proof that an offer needs a separate brand.

Draw the portfolio as a tree, then test journeys across it. Can someone move from a product page to the right support centre? Will a cross-sell feel like a useful connection or a surprise from an unrelated company? Does an acquisition have a clear transition path? The architecture should guide these moments, not merely look orderly on a wall.

### Build naming and visual rules

Define how names combine, shorten, and appear in text. Specify when a parent name is mandatory, how endorsements are phrased, and what happens when a product is mentioned alone. Then create a small visual grammar: hierarchy of marks, typography, colour, lock-ups, and spacing. Use examples across website navigation, product interface, proposal, invoice, and physical touchpoint.

Avoid solving every relationship with another colour or badge. Too many signals make the system harder to learn. Give designers room for distinct expression while retaining the cues that explain ownership and continuity.

## Plan for change

Test the model against plausible future cases: a new product, a merged service, a regional offering, and a discontinued name. Document criteria for creating a new sub-brand and a path for retiring one. Assign decision ownership; otherwise every launch becomes an exception that weakens the structure.

Roll out in stages. Update the most confusing public touchpoints first, while preserving redirects, search terms, and explanations that help existing customers find their way. Measure support questions, search behaviour, and comprehension rather than judging the change only by whether the brand diagram looks clean.

## Handoff and common mistake

Deliver a current portfolio map, decision rules, approved names and lock-ups, examples in real contexts, and a change log. State who approves new entries and when the map must be reviewed.

The common mistake is treating brand architecture as a logo arrangement exercise. A tidy chart cannot repair an unclear offer. The structure should make relationships easier for people to understand and easier for the organisation to maintain.
