## What it is

Brand guidelines are an operating manual for an identity. They explain which parts of a [visual identity system](/wiki/identity-and-branding/visual-identity-systems/) create recognition, how those parts behave across formats, and where informed variation is welcome. A logo sheet and a list of colour values are useful assets, but they do not answer the questions a designer faces when the next brief arrives.

The intended readers matter. An in-house designer may need component files and motion behaviour; a printer needs production values and minimum sizes; a partner may need a short set of approved examples. One giant document can serve as an index, but the working guidance should be easy to find at the moment of use.

## Begin with real decisions

Audit the identity in use before writing rules. Collect live examples from websites, presentations, packaging, social content, documents, and physical environments. Ask who creates each asset, who approves it, and where people currently improvise. Repeated workarounds often reveal a missing rule rather than a careless user.

Write a short statement of the brand's communication job. What should an audience recognise first? What tone should remain consistent when the message changes? Which parts are essential for recognition, and which are specific to a campaign or channel? This gives the guide a reason to exist beyond visual tidiness.

## Separate fixed rules from flexible choices

### Protect the recognition anchors

Document the mark, type system, colour relationships, image direction, compositional devices, and voice that should remain recognisable. Show acceptable variants, clear space, minimum sizes, and uses that fail. Explain the reason for a rule: a minimum size may protect legibility; a particular pairing may preserve contrast or brand recognition.

Describe relationships, not isolated values. For example, a colour may be a large background field but a poor choice for small text. A display face may carry campaign headlines while body copy uses a calmer family. A [logo guide](/wiki/identity-and-branding/logo-and-symbol-design/) should connect its usage rules to the broader system rather than repeat the same diagram on every page.

### Define a safe range of variation

Show how the system handles short and long headlines, unfamiliar photography, co-branding, translation, small screens, low-cost printing, and an urgent announcement. Include both ordinary and difficult examples. If only the polished hero applications work, the guide is incomplete.

Name the decisions users can make independently. Perhaps imagery may change by audience while the grid stays stable, or campaign colour can expand within an approved contrast range. This distinction prevents every new format from becoming a request for permission.

## Make the guide usable at work

Organise by task: choose assets, compose a layout, write a message, export a file, or request an exception. Place a short “start here” path ahead of deeper specifications. Supply editable source files, templates, export presets where relevant, and a clear location for current masters. Link to components and examples instead of copying files into several documents that will drift apart.

Pair each rule with at least one good example and, where helpful, a failure example. A rule such as “use generous space” becomes actionable when it shows a cramped poster beside a readable one at the same size. Give accessible alternatives for contrast, captions, and text over imagery. Do not present an inaccessible colour combination as a mandatory brand signature.

### Plan for production

Record colour specifications for the actual media, file formats, image rights, and who signs off printer or supplier proofs. A digital colour sample is not a guarantee of the result on fabric, uncoated paper, a backlit sign, or a low-end screen. Keep print and screen instructions adjacent to the examples that need them.

## Test the guidance with another person

Give the draft guide to someone who did not build the identity. Ask them to create a social tile, a document page, and one awkward new application using only the guide and its files. Note the decisions they cannot make, the assets they cannot find, and the places they interpret differently. Revise the guidance before calling it finished.

Governance is part of the design. Name an owner, a way to propose additions, a version date, and a change log. Review new applications regularly; accept useful patterns into the system and retire guidance that no longer reflects actual use. A living guide needs clear authority without becoming a bottleneck.

## Practical checks

- Can a new collaborator find the current logo, type, colour, and template files quickly?
- Do examples include long copy, mobile formats, and a limited-production situation?
- Are accessible colour and type choices shown in the contexts where people need them?
- Is it clear who approves a new pattern and which version is current?

## Common mistake

Treating the guideline PDF as the final deliverable. The real result is that a person can make the next correct decision without the original designer standing beside them. Test that ability, then keep the guide in step with the work.
