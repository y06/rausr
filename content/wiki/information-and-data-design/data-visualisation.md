## What it is

Data visualisation helps people compare values, detect patterns, inspect uncertainty, and understand relationships. It may support open-ended exploration or communicate a specific finding; the purpose determines the structure.

## Choose an encoding that fits the question

Position along a common scale is usually easier to compare precisely than area, angle, or colour intensity. Bar charts support discrete comparisons, lines show change across an ordered sequence, scatter plots reveal relationships, and maps add geographic structure. No chart type is automatically best outside a question and dataset.

## Preserve the data relationship

Scales, baselines, intervals, aggregation, missing values, and filtering can change the apparent conclusion. Visual emphasis should reflect the evidence rather than decorate it. Explain transformations that a reader would need in order to interpret the result correctly.

## Add context

Titles, units, source notes, annotations, definitions, and comparison values help a chart answer a real question. Labels placed near the data usually reduce the effort of moving between a legend and the marks.

## Practical checks

- State what is being measured, in which units, and over what period.
- Include the source and relevant methodological notes.
- Test colour choices for contrast and colour-vision differences.
- Remove effects that suggest depth or precision not present in the data.
- Check the chart at mobile width and with long labels.

## Common mistake

Starting with a visually impressive chart form and forcing the data into it. Begin with the comparison the reader needs to make, then choose the simplest encoding that supports that comparison honestly.
